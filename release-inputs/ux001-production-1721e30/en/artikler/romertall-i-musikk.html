Roman numerals: the chords behind the numbers

Roman numerals describe relationships rather than fixed chord names. Learn the numbers first, then use them to understand chords in a key, transpose progressions and hear where the music is heading, wherever you play.

WHAT YOU WILL LEARN

  • Read Roman numerals I to X and explain why IV means four.
  • Identify a chord’s scale degree and distinguish major, minor and diminished.
  • Turn a Roman-numeral progression into chords in different keys.
  • Distinguish Roman numerals, chord names and inversions.

Before you start

You should know what a key, a major scale and a major or minor triad are. Start with the articles on intervals and chords if needed.

First: a short introduction to Roman numerals

Roman numerals are an older number system also used in music theory. Before analysing any chords, learn three signs: I means 1, V means 5 and X means 10. Combine them to write other numbers.

Roman numeralNumberHow to remember it
I1One unit
II2Two units
III3Three units
IV4One before five: 5 − 1
V5Five
VI6Five plus one
VII7Five plus two
VIII8Five plus three
IX9One before ten: 10 − 1
X10Ten

A smaller sign before a larger one is subtracted: IV = 4 and IX = 9. After it, the smaller sign is added: VI = 6. In ordinary functional harmony we mainly use I to VII, because we build chords on the seven scale degrees.

The chord name tells you what; the numeral tells you where

Take C major: C–D–E–F–G–A–B. Build a triad on each degree by taking alternate notes. C–E–G gives C major, D–F–A gives D minor, and so on.

A chord name such as G identifies the notes forming the chord. A Roman numeral such as V locates the chord’s root within the key being analysed. G is V in C major but I in G major. That is why the system is useful.

Uppercase and lowercase matter

We use an important convention: uppercase numerals indicate major triads (I, IV, V), while lowercase numerals indicate minor triads (ii, iii, vi). A small circle, °, means diminished: vii°. A plus sign, +, indicates an augmented triad, for example III+.

Thus II and ii are different. Both have their root on degree two, but II is major and ii is minor. In C major, Dm is the diatonic ii. D major may appear as II, but contains F♯ and lies outside the key’s usual diatonic triads. We would often investigate whether it has a particular harmonic function.

Remember: The numeral identifies the scale degree of the chord’s root. Letter case indicates chord quality. These are separate pieces of information.

Move the progression; keep the relationship

Take C–Am–F–G. In C major this is I–vi–IV–V. Now move it to G major, whose notes are G–A–B–C–D–E–F♯. The same degrees give G–Em–C–D. The chord names change, but the numerals remain.

KeyIviIVV
C majorCAmFG
G majorGEmCD
D majorDBmGA

This is transposition in practice. Instead of memorising a song as disconnected chord names, understand the relationships. On guitar choose new shapes; on piano find the new chord tones. Roman numerals provide a shared language.

What do i–iv–V and ii–V–I mean?

Use the numerals to understand two common progressions. In C major, ii–V–I = Dm–G–C, or Dm7–G7–Cmaj7 with sevenths. The roots are on degrees two, five and one. The final chord is home in this key. The numerals describe chord relationships; they do not automatically determine an improvisation mode.

In A minor, i–iv–V = Am–Dm–E. Notice that E is major. A natural minor contains G and would give Em as diatonic v. Raising G to G♯ gives E major a stronger dominant pull. That is why V is common in minor-key harmony. Analyse the actual chord and its musical context.

Play C–F–G–C slowly and notice G’s pull towards C. Then play Dm–G–C. You reach the same home from a different starting point.

Sevenths, inversions and common misreadings

Numerals can show more detail. V7 is a seventh chord on degree five, such as G7 in C major. The 7 describes the chord’s added seventh, not a move to scale degree seven. vii° is a diminished triad on degree seven; the circle is not a zero.

In traditional figured-bass and Roman-numeral analysis, I6 usually means a first-inversion tonic triad, such as C/E. It does not automatically mean a C6 chord with added A. I6/4 means second inversion, C/G. Some modern pop and jazz charts use other systems; check the notation being used.

Similarly, I–IV–V does not tell you to play Ionian, Lydian and Mixolydian in turn. It identifies chords relative to a key. Mode labels and Roman numerals answer different musical questions.

Textbooks vary in notation, particularly for seventh chords and chromatic harmony. Here we use uppercase/lowercase for major/minor within ordinary functional analysis. Check the key first.

Our method: hear → predict → play → explain → change

Hear: Play I–IV–V–I in C major and listen for the return home. Predict: Name I, IV and V in G major before playing. Play: Check on guitar or piano. Explain: Why is D major V in G major but II in C major? Change: Transpose I–vi–IV–V into another key and write a short melody landing on a chord tone at each change.

This takes you from knowing the signs to using harmony. Roman numerals are not music by themselves; they reveal patterns in what you hear and play.

References: Open Music Theory – Roman Numerals and musictheory.net – Roman Numeral Analysis: Triads. The explanations and practice sequence are adapted for MODUS.

FROM READING TO PLAYING

Try it yourself.

Analyse C–Am–F–G in C major as Roman numerals. Transpose it to G major without consulting the table. Check on guitar or piano and explain why Em is vi in G major.

  1. Read: Say I, II, III, IV, V, VI, VII and translate them into 1–7.
  2. Build: Find C, Dm, Em, F, G, Am and Bdim on the instrument and label each correctly.
  3. Move: Play I–vi–IV–V in C and G major. Listen and explain what stays the same.
